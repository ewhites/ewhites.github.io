Two pendulums, 0.001° apart

If you hang a pendulum off the bottom of another pendulum, you get a really simple machine that's nearly impossible to predict. I wanted to see just how fast that happens, so I built this.

What's going on here

There are two double pendulums here, blue and red. Both have two 1-meter arms and two 1-kilogram weights, and both start at rest with their arms held out at 120°. The only difference is that the red one's top arm starts 0.001° further out. At the end of a 1-meter arm, that's about 17 micrometers (thinner than a human hair).

For the first few seconds they look identical. Then the red one starts drifting, and pretty soon they're doing totally different things. The chart under the pendulums tracks the gap between them. It uses a log scale, so each gridline is 10 or 100 times bigger than the one below it. That's why the line climbs steadily even though the gap is exploding.

Why does this happen?

Every time the top arm swings, it tugs on the bottom arm. How hard it tugs depends on exactly where both arms are. So if the red pendulum is a tiny bit off, it gets a slightly different tug, which puts it a bit more off, which changes the next tug even more. Small errors keep building on themselves.

Physicists call this chaos. The funny thing is there's nothing random going on at all. If you knew the starting position perfectly, you could predict the pendulum forever. You just can't ever know it perfectly, and the pendulum blows up whatever tiny error you have.

The numbers

I ran the same simulation in Python with the same settings and timed how long it took for the two bottom arms to end up more than 10° apart:

Start angleStarting gapGap passed 10° at
120°0.1°4.74 s
120°0.001°7.74 s
120°0.000001°12.45 s
20°0.001°never (0.0028° after 60 s)

My favorite part is the 120° rows. Starting 1,000 times more precisely (0.001° down to 0.000001°) only bought me 4.71 more seconds before they split. The gap grew 10,000x in 7.74 seconds, so it roughly doubles every 0.6 seconds.

At 20° it's a different story. Small swings make the double pendulum act a lot like two normal pendulums, and after a full minute the gap only went from 0.001° to 0.0028°. It needs enough energy before the chaos kicks in.

Weather works the same way, which is part of why forecasts get worse the further out they go. Better measurements help, but each big jump in precision only buys a little more time.

How I built it

For each pendulum, the computer keeps track of four numbers: the angle of each arm and how fast each arm is spinning. Newton's laws give you an equation for how those speeds change. Here they are (from myPhysicsLab), where θ₁ and θ₂ are the arm angles, ω is how fast each one spins, and the dots mean "rate of change":

θ̈₁ = [ −g(2m₁+m₂)sin θ₁ − m₂g sin(θ₁−2θ₂)
       − 2 sin(θ₁−θ₂) m₂ (ω₂²L₂ + ω₁²L₁ cos(θ₁−θ₂)) ]
     / [ L₁(2m₁ + m₂ − m₂ cos(2θ₁−2θ₂)) ]

θ̈₂ = [ 2 sin(θ₁−θ₂) ( ω₁²L₁(m₁+m₂) + g(m₁+m₂) cos θ₁
       + ω₂²L₂m₂ cos(θ₁−θ₂) ) ]
     / [ L₂(2m₁ + m₂ − m₂ cos(2θ₁−2θ₂)) ]

The simulation moves forward 1 millisecond at a time using 4th-order Runge-Kutta. Instead of guessing the next position from one slope, it checks the slope four times within each step and takes a weighted average. It's a lot more accurate than the simple approach for the same step size.

To make sure the computer wasn't just making things up, I tracked the total energy. With no friction, it should never change, so any change is error from the simulation. At 120°, energy drifted by less than one part in 100 million over 60 seconds (the "Energy error" readout shows this live). So the red and blue pendulums splitting apart is real physics, not a rounding error.

Things to try

  • Drag the starting angle down to 20°. The gap line on the chart barely moves.
  • Set the starting gap to 0.000001° and watch the "passed 10°" time. It only goes from about 7.7 s to about 12.5 s.
  • Try 170°. It starts almost upside down and goes crazy right away.
